## 2. The script

**315 spoken words** with the "not yet" result line (**321** with the live result line). At a calm 140 words a minute that is about 2 minutes 15 seconds of talking. Sorty's own voice in the demo adds about 20 seconds and the clicks about 10, so the whole thing lands near **2:45**, and near 2:55 if you speak slowly. Time it once with a stopwatch: that is the real check.

Lines starting with `>` are spoken. Lines in [brackets] are what happens on screen. One speaker can do all of it. With two, Speaker 1 takes beats 1, 2, 3 and 6, and Speaker 2 runs the demo and beat 5.

### Beat 1. Introduce your project (0:00 to 0:14)

[Showcase page open on the station, idle face on Sorty's screen.]

> Hi, we are [team name]. This is Sorty: a camera, a screen and a voice that fit on any bin and tell you where your trash goes. Our track is Off the Grid.

### Beat 2. Explain the problem (0:14 to 0:35)

> In 2020, the City of Tampa reported that 36 percent of what people put in recycling was not recyclable. The city says food and liquid turn recycling into trash. Signs do not fix this, because the answer is often hidden. Is this pizza box greasy inside? Is this can empty?

### Beat 3. Describe your solution (0:35 to 0:57)

> One picture cannot tell. Sorty knows what it cannot see, and it has four moves. Visible? It tells you. Hidden on another side? It asks you to show it. Too small? It looks closer. Impossible to see? It asks one short question. Three requests at most, because nobody waits at a bin.

### Beat 4. Demonstrate its main features (0:57 to 1:51)

[Speaker 2 can take over here.]

[Pick the closed pizza box in the showcase.]

> Here is our virtual station, with a closed pizza box.

[Sorty says: "Open the box and show me inside." Let the judge hear it.]

> It cannot see inside, so it asks.

[The box opens. Sorty says: "Green bin, in the middle. Grease spoils paper recycling." The green bin lights up.]

> Now it sees the grease: the green bin lights up, with one short reason.

[Pick the soda can. Sorty says: "Is there liquid inside?"]

> A soda can: no camera sees through metal, so it asks me.

[Answer Yes. Sorty says: "First, pour out the soda. Then: blue bin, on your left."]

[Pick the batteries. Sorty says: "Not in these bins. Please use the battery drop-off box."]

> Batteries: no bin, because they can start fires.

[Open the "How Sorty decided" panel.]

> This panel shows every step.

**If the real AI is connected:** each step takes a few seconds longer. Do the pizza box and the soda can only, and skip the batteries.

**If the walk-through is scripted** (the screen shows a PRACTICE badge): say it out loud once, right at the start of this beat: "This walk-through is scripted." Beat 5 then says the rest.

The bin rules are one small file, so the same brain runs three bins or two. We ran 107 automatic checks on our code and wrote 32 test cases with a hidden truth.

## 4. Likely questions

The guide says the judge's minute covers "functionality, technical implementation, and design decisions".

**Know this by heart (every teammate):** a camera picture goes from the screen page to a small server on the laptop. The server sends it to a vision AI model with the bin rules. The model must answer with one of four moves. Our code checks that move, enforces the limit of three, and turns it into what the screen shows and the voice says.

**Functionality**

1. **Is the AI answering live right now, and how accurate is it?**
   If scripted: "No. This walk-through is scripted, and the live run with the real AI is still to do, so we have no accuracy number." If live: "Yes. On our 32 test cases it got [__] right, and those pictures are clean renders, so a real camera will be harder."

2. **What happens if the person does not answer, does not know, or lies?**
   "Sorty gives the answer that is safe either way, like compost for a pizza box it could not see inside, because clean cardboard does no harm there. What a person says reaches the AI as information, never as a command, and our test set includes someone who tries to trick it."

3. **What does it take to put this on a real bin, and what does it cost?**
   "Any USB camera, a small computer, a screen and a speaker, plus one file that describes the bins. We have not built the hardware or measured the cost per item yet."

**Technical implementation**

4. **Which AI model do you use, and why not Gemini?**
   "The brain uses Anthropic's Claude vision model through its official software kit, and each step it has to pick one of four tools. The model call sits in one file, so another vision model would need a new adapter there; today only Claude is built."

5. **What did you build yourselves, and what did AI generate?**
   "We chose the problem and designed the four moves, the bin rules, the limits and the 32 test cases, and we directed the work. An AI coding assistant, Claude Code, wrote most of the code and the Blender scripts, and our README says so." [Change this to match what each of you really did. It must agree with the README.]

6. **How is this different from smart bins that already exist?**
   "Camera bins that classify one picture already exist. As far as we know they answer from that single look; our new part is knowing what cannot be seen, then looking again or asking."

**Design decisions**

7. **Why ask questions at all? People are impatient.**
   "A warning is enough when the bin is the same either way, but when the hidden fact changes the bin, a warning hands the choice back to the person, which is what signs already do. So Sorty asks only then, never more than three times, and we have discussed a version that answers first and makes the question optional; that version is not built."

8. **A camera in a public place: what happens to the pictures?**
   "Sorty keeps a picture in memory for one item and does not save it, and the screen says so. The picture is sent to the AI service to be read, so a real installation would need a clear notice for that."

In 2020, the City of Tampa reported that 36 percent of what people put in recycling was not recyclable, often because of hidden food or liquid. One picture cannot see that. Sorty knows what it cannot see. It asks you to open the box, or asks one short question. Then it lights the right bin and tells you why.
