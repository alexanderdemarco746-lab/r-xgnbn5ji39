## 2. The script

**318 spoken words.** The talking outside the demo is 262 words, about 1 minute 52 seconds at a calm 140 words a minute. The live demo of two items takes about 50 seconds, and you talk over the waiting. That lands near **2:40 to 2:45**, and near 2:50 if you speak slowly. The 50 seconds is my estimate from today's measured AI times plus the animation; the showcase page itself has not been timed with the live AI. ### Optional: the camera moment

This can be the strongest moment of the demo, and it is the riskiest. Keep it out of the timed three minutes. Offer it during the questions, or after: "Do you have a bottle or a cup? Let Sorty look at it."

How it works on the page:

1. Click **Use my camera** and allow the camera. The button changes to "Camera on".
2. Hold the object up to the webcam.
3. Press the blue **Sensor: object in range** button, or the Space key. Say what it is: "This button stands in for the distance sensor a real bin would have."
4. If Sorty asks to open or turn the object, do it, then press the same button, which now reads "Done: look again".
5. If Sorty asks a question, press Yes, No or Not sure under the scene.
6. Click "Camera on" again to go back to the cards.

The risks, all real:

- **It has not been tried with a real webcam.** Try it with two real objects before judging. If it fails then, leave it out.
- A real object in a real room is harder than our rendered pictures. Sorty may get it wrong in front of the judge.
- One object takes 10 to 25 seconds and a few cents of the budget.
- It works only on the live AI. In practice mode the answers are scripted and have nothing to do with the object.

### If the showcase misbehaves

Use the kiosk screen by itself: click **Open the real screen** at the top of the showcase, or open `http://localhost:8787`. Press **M**, pick "Pizza box, greasy inside", then "Soda can, half full". Press **D** for the "How Sorty decided" panel. Keep `sorty/design/renders/01_hero.png` open in a second window and point at it when you say "our virtual station".

## 4. Likely questions

The guide says the judge's minute covers "functionality, technical implementation, and design decisions".

**Know this by heart (every teammate):** a camera picture goes from the screen page to a small server on the laptop. The server sends it to a vision AI model with the bin rules. The model must answer with one of four moves. For a small detail such as grease, one more focused check reads the picture. The code checks each move, enforces the limit of three, and turns it into what the screen shows and the voice says.

**Functionality**

1. **Is the AI answering live right now, and how accurate is it?**
   On the live AI: "Yes, every answer you saw came from the AI model just now. Today it sorted two items right in full runs, but that is two items: we have not run our 32 test cases, so we quote no accuracy number." In practice mode: "Not at this moment: this walk-through is scripted, as the badge says. Earlier today the live AI sorted these two items right, which is two items and not an accuracy number."

2. **Why does it take so long? How fast is it?**
   "After a one-time warm-up, the AI needed about 5 seconds for the can and about 12 for the pizza box, which includes the extra grease check. That is slower than we want at a bin, and the two cheaper models we tried were not faster, so speed is open work."

3. **What happens if the person does not answer, does not know, or lies?**
   "Sorty gives the answer that is safe either way, like compost for a pizza box it could not see inside, because clean cardboard does no harm there. What a person says reaches the AI as information, never as a command, and the test set includes someone who tries to trick it."

4. **What does it take to put this on a real bin?**
   "A small computer such as a Raspberry Pi, a camera, a small touch screen, a speaker and an LED strip, plus one file that describes the bins. None of that is built: the program that drives the LEDs is not written, and neither the voice nor the camera has been tried on that hardware."

5. **What does it cost?**
   "From parts on sale today, one kit is about 340 dollars. Running it: today's two items cost 3 to 5 cents each on the current model, and our estimate on the cheapest model is 13 to 20 dollars a month for one bin at 300 items a day; we tried that model on two items only."

**Technical implementation**

6. **Which AI model do you use, and why not Gemini?**
   "The brain uses Anthropic's Claude vision model through its official software kit, and each step it has to pick one of four tools. The model call sits in one file, so another vision model would need a new adapter there; today only Claude is built."

7. **What did you build yourselves, and what did AI generate?**
   "All the code, the Blender scripts and the written documents were made by an AI coding assistant, Claude Code, from our instructions, and our README lists that part by part. We chose the problem, said how Sorty must behave, such as the pizza box and soda can cases, made the product decisions and reviewed the work." [This matches the README's credits section. If you made something by hand, add it in both places.]

8. **How is this different from smart bins that already exist?**
   "Camera bins that classify one picture already exist. As far as we know they answer from that single look; the new part here is knowing what cannot be seen, then looking again or asking."

**Design decisions**

9. **Why ask questions at all? People are impatient.**
   "A warning is enough when the bin is the same either way, but when the hidden fact changes the bin, a warning hands the choice back to the person, which is what signs already do. So Sorty asks only then, never more than three times, and we have discussed a version that answers first and makes the question optional; that version is not built."

10. **A camera in a public place: what happens to the pictures?**
    "Sorty keeps a picture in memory for one item and does not save it, and the screen says so. The picture is sent to the AI service to be read, so a real installation would need a clear notice for that."
